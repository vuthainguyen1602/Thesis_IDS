\begin{abstract}
Deploying machine learning (ML) based intrusion detection on resource-constrained edge devices requires balancing accuracy, latency, and operational cost. We present a split-deployment IDS architecture across two NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super devices connected through Apache Kafka. The system implements a two-stage pipeline: (1) a lightweight autoencoder anomaly gate on Jetson~\#1 filters benign traffic, and (2) a PySpark \texttt{PipelineModel} classifier on Jetson~\#2 processes only suspicious flows. Central infrastructure (Kafka, PostgreSQL, InfluxDB, Grafana) runs on a host PC. The classifier is the model selected by our companion Spark ML study under a leakage-aware evaluation (label-leaking port features removed, feature-level duplicate flows eliminated). We evaluate three distributed modes (pipeline split, horizontal Kafka consumer scaling, and a Spark standalone cluster) while streaming CICIDS2017 network flows. Following every flow from send to verdict, queueing included, pipeline split sustains 22 flows/s against 2.5 for a single node running the whole pipeline ($\approx$9$\times$), at an end-to-end p95 of about 7\,s and 0.60\,J per verdict against 3.04\,J. The price is recall: the gate skips 89\% of flows and, at its pre-committed threshold, lets about 30\% of attack flows through as benign.

\keywords{Intrusion detection \and Edge computing \and Jetson Orin Nano Super \and Kafka \and PySpark \and Stream processing.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Network intrusion detection systems (IDS) increasingly must operate closer to data sources to reduce bandwidth and response time~\cite{shi2016edge,hamdan2020edge}. Recent work confirms that accurate detectors now fit on single-board hardware: ensemble and metaheuristic-optimized detectors have been demonstrated on IoT-edge platforms~\cite{baalaji2025ensemble,asghar2026hedid}, lightweight models have been designed for deployment on industrial control devices~\cite{zhang2026edgeics}, and NVIDIA Jetson-class boards have been used both for autoencoder-based IIoT detection~\cite{hasan2025iiot} and, on the Jetson Orin Nano specifically, for generative-AI-assisted traffic classification~\cite{diazgorrin2026jetson}. Such devices nonetheless offer limited CPU, memory, and thermal headroom, so a monolithic detector that classifies every flow saturates the board quickly.

Three gaps persist in this literature. First, the recent edge-IDS studies above evaluate a \emph{single} device: streaming middleware such as Kafka has been paired with Spark for distributed detection~\cite{kumar2025kafkaspark}, but the resulting pipelines are not measured on a multi-node cluster of constrained edge boards with explicit role separation, so it remains unclear how much a second board actually buys and in which distribution mode. Second, energy is now a design constraint in its own right at the edge~\cite{nassef2026tinyml}, yet throughput, latency percentiles, and per-inference energy are rarely reported together for one deployed system. Third, offline detection scores are increasingly under scrutiny: methodological pitfalls in security ML~\cite{arp2022dosdonts} and documented labeling and duplication errors in CICIDS2017~\cite{lanvin2023errors} mean that a model selected under a leaky protocol may be optimistic by several F1 points before it is ever deployed.

We address all three at once. A practical edge IDS must decide \emph{which} model to deploy (small, accurate, explainable, and selected under a leakage-aware protocol) and \emph{how} to distribute inference across nodes under streaming load. We therefore split detection into a lightweight autoencoder gate and a heavier PySpark classifier placed on separate Jetson Orin Nano Super boards, and we measure the resulting system end to end.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1) A two-stage distributed IDS combining a lightweight anomaly gate with a Spark-based classifier;
(2) Three deployment modes for a two-node Jetson cluster (pipeline split, horizontal scaling, Spark standalone cluster);
(3) An end-to-end evaluation that follows every flow from send to verdict, reporting sustained capacity, latency percentiles with queueing included, live attack recall, and energy per verdict, metrics that offline studies typically omit;
(4) Open, reproducible scripts integrated with a leakage-aware Spark ML training pipeline, so the deployed model is the one validated under a rigorous offline protocol; all code and configurations are publicly available\footnote{\url{https://github.com/vuthainguyen1602/Thesis_IDS} (branch \texttt{develop}).}.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Edge and on-device intrusion detection.} Edge computing pushes analytics closer to data sources to reduce latency and bandwidth~\cite{shi2016edge,hamdan2020edge}, and successive work has shrunk IDS models until they fit single-board hardware. Early systems such as RPiDS~\cite{sforzin2016rpids} showed that a complete detection stack could run on a Raspberry Pi at all; recent studies instead optimize the detector itself, via deep ensemble stacking on IoT-edge platforms~\cite{baalaji2025ensemble}, metaheuristic hyper-parameter search combined with explainability~\cite{asghar2026hedid}, and compact models tailored to industrial control traffic~\cite{zhang2026edgeics}. NVIDIA Jetson boards are a recurring target: autoencoder-based feature learning reaches sub-millisecond inference on a Jetson Nano~\cite{hasan2025iiot}, and generative models have been run on the Jetson Orin Nano to classify traffic and synthesize training data at once~\cite{diazgorrin2026jetson}. These studies share a single-device assumption: the detector is benchmarked in isolation on one board, so how detection work should be placed across several boards, and what a second board actually buys, remain open.

\textbf{Streaming and distributed detection pipelines.} Kafka~\cite{kreps2011kafka} decouples producers from consumers and has become the de-facto ingest layer for streaming analytics, while Spark~\cite{zaharia2010spark} and its MLlib library~\cite{meng2016mllib} supply the distributed training and scoring primitives. Combining the two for intrusion detection is an established pattern; Mohan Kiran Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2025kafkaspark}, for instance, route Kafka streams into Spark classifiers for distributed detection. Such pipelines are conceived for datacenter-class resources, where an additional executor is cheap, whereas on an 8\,GB ARM board the JVM footprint is itself a design constraint, and the corresponding per-node CPU, memory, and energy traces are rarely published. More broadly, ML-based IDS surveys~\cite{khraisat2019survey,ferrag2020deep,vinayakumar2019deep} catalog detection accuracy across dozens of models but seldom report the end-to-end throughput or latency percentiles of a running deployment.

\textbf{Two-stage and split inference.} Anomaly-detection surveys~\cite{chandola2009anomaly,ruff2021unifying} systematize the family of detectors that model normal behavior alone; this property is what makes a cascaded design attractive, in which a cheap unsupervised stage filters the bulk of normal data and a costlier supervised stage adjudicates the remainder. Unsupervised deep detectors, deep belief networks~\cite{alrawashdeh2016toward} and autoencoders~\cite{lopez2017autoencoder,hasan2025iiot}, are the usual choice for the cheap stage, since they can be calibrated on benign traffic alone. Distributing detection across devices has so far been explored mainly along the federated-learning axis, where nodes collaborate during training while inference stays local~\cite{nguyen2019diot,hernandezramos2025federated}. Our split is orthogonal and concerns inference: the two stages are physically separated across boards and joined by a Kafka topic, so gate and classifier scale independently, and no prior work applies a PySpark MLlib classifier in this role on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super.\footnote{\url{https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/autonomous-machines/embedded-systems/jetson-orin/nano-super-developer-kit/}} Placement matters most for energy: recent TinyML work argues that energy per inference is a first-class edge metric~\cite{nassef2026tinyml}, and skipping the heavy stage is the most direct way to lower it.

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.} A parallel line of work questions how faithfully offline scores transfer to deployment. Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022dosdonts} catalog recurring pitfalls in security ML, and Lanvin et al.~\cite{lanvin2023errors} document labeling and duplication errors in CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018toward} that measurably inflate reported performance; Sarhan et al.~\cite{sarhan2022explainable} similarly show that the choice of standard feature set shapes both generalizability and explainability. Our companion ML study responds by selecting a leakage-free SHAP Top-30 model~\cite{lundberg2017unified}, and the present paper deploys exactly that model, so the operational numbers reported here do not belong to a configuration that would fail a stricter offline audit. Concept drift~\cite{gama2014survey} lies outside the present scope and motivates the retraining direction we outline in the conclusion. Table~\ref{tab:related-work} positions our system against representative related work along the five axes above: multi-node role separation, streaming ingest, joint energy and latency reporting, leakage-aware model selection, and open code.

\subsection{Pipeline Roles}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anomaly gate:} a lightweight autoencoder + scaler over all 77 leak-free flow features; the MSE threshold is calibrated offline as a quantile (0.995) of a held-out benign validation split (10\% of benign training flows, never the test set), so the operating point is committed before evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Classifier:} PySpark \texttt{PipelineModel} with the leakage-free SHAP-selected feature set.
  \item \textbf{Combined role:} gate and classifier in one process, used for horizontal scaling via Kafka consumer groups.
\end{itemize}
Each tier scores flows in batches of 20, or sooner once the oldest waiting flow has been held for 1\,s, so the classifier, which receives only a small share of traffic, never holds a partial batch indefinitely.

\subsection{Monitoring and Alerting}

Each node tags its metrics with a unique node identifier in InfluxDB and PostgreSQL. Alerts are emitted from a designated primary node only, avoiding duplicate notifications. Fig.~\ref{fig:grafana} shows the live Grafana dashboard while CICIDS2017 flows are replayed through the split deployment: prediction throughput, detected-attack count, inference latency, and per-node CPU/RAM utilization are all tracked in real time, alongside the most recent attack alerts persisted to PostgreSQL. Fig.~\ref{fig:grafana-peak} captures the dashboard at peak load in an earlier run with the previous model: the gate node idles at 4.6\% CPU while the PySpark classifier node reaches 90.8\%, so the classifier dominates the compute budget.

\section{Implementation}

Training and model export run on a Spark standalone cluster (host PC as master, two Jetson Orin Nano Super devices as workers) using Apache Spark~\cite{zaharia2010spark} and its MLlib library~\cite{meng2016mllib}; the cluster topology is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:spark-cluster}. The host only coordinates (Spark master) and does not run an executor, so all reported training compute reflects the two homogeneous Jetson workers rather than the stronger host CPU. The edge stack is implemented in Python with configurable per-node roles. Every offline run logs its configuration (random seed, leakage/dedup switches, library versions) so each exported model is traceable to the exact pipeline that produced it.

\subsection{Offline Phase (Spark ML), Leakage-Aware}

In our companion ML study, we evaluate four feature configurations (baseline with all features, RF Importance Top-30, SHAP Top-30, and PCA with $k$=40) under a fixed 80/20 train--test split, using eight Spark ML classifiers and statistical validation (repeated paired resampling and permutation tests). PCA uses $k$=40 rather than 30 because it extracts components (linear combinations spreading variance across many axes) instead of selecting original features, so it needs more components to retain comparable variance; across the common $\{20,30,40\}$ sweep, $k$=40 retains the most variance and is the fairest representative operating point for PCA. LightGBM is excluded because its SynapseML native libraries are x86\_64-only and cannot run on the ARM64 Jetson Orin Nano Super deployment target. The offline protocol is leakage-aware~\cite{arp2022dosdonts}: it removes information that would be unavailable at deployment time but that otherwise inflates test scores. Motivated by documented labeling and duplication errors in CICIDS2017~\cite{lanvin2023errors}, the label-leaking \texttt{destination\_port} feature is removed and flows that are identical on all feature columns are de-duplicated before splitting. This yields realistic F1 values (Table~\ref{tab:spark-edge-bridge}) rather than the near-perfect scores obtained when these leaks are left in place. Offline metrics constrain which exported model is worth deploying; they are not directly comparable to edge throughput or latency.

\subsection{Edge Phase (Jetson Orin Nano Super)}

The exported \texttt{PipelineModel} (VectorAssembler, StandardScaler, classifier) is loaded on Jetson~\#2 with only the selected SHAP features, reducing assembly and inference cost relative to the 77-feature baseline. The deployed input vector contains the 30 SHAP-selected features; the ranking is computed after \texttt{destination\_port} has been removed as a label-leaking channel, consistent with the leakage-aware offline protocol. A lightweight autoencoder gate on Jetson~\#1 filters benign flows before they reach Spark inference, further lowering load on the classifier node. End-to-end edge evaluation measures sustained throughput (flows/s), send-to-verdict latency p50/p95, live attack recall, gate-skip ratio, and energy per verdict, none of which the offline Spark experiments capture.

\subsection{Division of Evaluation Metrics}

Table~\ref{tab:metrics-split} clarifies which metric belongs to which phase. Mixing offline F1 with edge latency in a single ranking would conflate accuracy with operational feasibility.

\section{Experimental Setup}

The testbed comprises two NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kits and a host PC running Docker Compose, connected over a Wi-Fi LAN. Each Jetson board pairs a 6-core Arm Cortex-A78AE CPU with an NVIDIA Ampere GPU featuring 1024 CUDA and 32 Tensor cores, delivering roughly 67 TOPS, and is equipped with 8\,GB of LPDDR5 memory and a 256\,GB NVMe SSD. On the software side, streaming inference runs on PySpark 3.4.1 over Apache Kafka 3.5 (Confluent Platform 7.5.0), while InfluxDB and Grafana collect and visualize runtime metrics. The workload replays 10{,}000 flows sampled from the CICIDS2017 test split (15.2\% attacks, never seen in training). We compare a single node running the full pipeline, with and without the gate, against the three distributed modes A (pipeline split), B (horizontal Kafka scaling, full role on both boards) and C (Spark standalone cluster, host as master).

Every verdict stores the flow's producer timestamp and replay row, so each flow sent during a load window is followed to its verdict wherever it is issued. We report \emph{sustained capacity}, the highest offered rate at which every flow sent in a 45\,s window receives its verdict within 10\,s of the window's end (about one PySpark batch); send-to-verdict latency p50/p95 over those flows, queueing included (NTP-synchronized clocks); the gate-skip ratio and attack recall computed from the flows themselves; and energy per verdict from \texttt{tegrastats} at the sustained rate. Each rate is run twice after a warm-up, with a fresh Kafka consumer group per run so no run inherits another's backlog.

\textbf{Capacity.} Pipeline split sustains 22.4 flows/s, about $9\times$ the single node that runs the whole pipeline (2.5\,flows/s). The gain comes from placing the two stages on separate boards, not from the gate alone: with the gate added on the same board, the single node sustains only 1.9\,flows/s, because gate scoring and the Spark classifier compete for the same CPU. Horizontal scaling of the full role (gate off) stays Spark-bound at 3.3\,flows/s, and the Spark standalone cluster reaches 8.7\,flows/s, less than half of pipeline split, at the cost of an extra host dependency.

\textbf{Latency.} At its sustained rate, pipeline split returns a median verdict in about 1\,s, dominated by gate-only verdicts; flows forwarded to the classifier wait for a PySpark batch, and the p95 over all flows is 7.1\,s. Every mode shows a multi-second tail for the same reason: one PySpark batch on the board takes seconds regardless of its size.

\textbf{Energy efficiency.} Power is sampled with \texttt{tegrastats} (30\,s idle baseline, then the load window). Serving 18.4\,flows/s on two boards, pipeline split spends 0.60\,J per verdict against 3.04\,J for a single node at its 2\,flows/s, a $5\times$ reduction; the active (idle-subtracted) power rises by about 1.5\,W under load in every mode.

\textbf{Inference-engine baseline (cost of the unified stack).} We deploy a Spark \texttt{PipelineModel} on the edge for consistency with the distributed training phase: the same stack trains on the cluster and serves on the device. To quantify the price of that unification, we export the deployed forest to NumPy and ONNX Runtime and serve all three through the edge's own inference layer on the classifier node, batch size 10 (Table~\ref{tab:engines}). The three engines return identical predictions on every replayed flow. ONNX Runtime serves the same forest at about 10{,}100\,flows/s, about $7{,}200\times$ the PySpark path, at $<$0.01\% of its active energy per inference. The gap motivates exporting the \texttt{PipelineModel} to ONNX/TensorRT for serving while retaining Spark for training; wiring the ONNX session into the streaming pipeline is left as future work.

\textbf{Gate trade-off.} The anomaly gate is tunable rather than a single fixed point: sweeping its threshold trades attack recall against the fraction of flows offloaded from Spark (Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}). \section{Discussion}

Pipeline split relieves the Spark classifier of about 89\% of the traffic, and that is what lifts sustained capacity from 2.5 to 22.4\,flows/s. Two findings qualify the design. First, the placement matters as much as the filter: the same gate on the same board as the classifier gains nothing, so the benefit of a second board comes from role separation. Second, the throughput is paid for in recall: at the pre-committed threshold about 30\% of attack flows are skipped as benign, so an operator trading recall for capacity should do so knowingly along the curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}.

As summarized in Sect.~\ref{sec:bridge}, offline Spark experiments and edge Jetson benchmarks answer different questions. Feature reduction from 77 to 30 dimensions (SHAP Top-30) directly reduces \texttt{VectorAssembler} and \texttt{PipelineModel} cost on the Orin Nano Super, while the anomaly gate skips most flows before Spark inference is invoked. These two choices connect the accuracy-oriented conclusions of the companion ML study to a deployable, measurable edge architecture.

\textbf{Limitations.} Benchmarks are obtained on a two-node lab cluster over Wi-Fi; wired networking and larger clusters may shift latency tails. Capacity is bounded by the PySpark classifier, whose per-batch cost of several seconds sets both the tail latency and the ceiling; the $\sim$67-TOPS GPU remains unexploited by the CPU-only pipeline. The gate bounds recall, and its threshold is calibrated on benign training data only; live-traffic re-calibration is future work. Energy figures subtract a single pre-run idle baseline, so within-run drift in background power is not captured. Results are tied to CICIDS2017 replay: live traffic and deliberately evasive (adversarial) traffic remain untested.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a reproducible distributed edge IDS for Jetson Orin Nano Super clusters that pairs Kafka streaming with PySpark inference and deploys a model validated under a leakage-aware offline protocol. Measured from send to verdict with queueing included, placing a lightweight autoencoder gate and the Spark classifier on separate boards sustains 22.4 flows/s against 2.5 for a single node running the whole pipeline ($\approx$9$\times$), at an end-to-end p95 of about 7\,s and 0.60\,J per verdict against 3.04\,J. The gain requires the separate board: the same gate on the classifier's board sustains 1.9\,flows/s. It is paid for in recall, since the gate lets about 30\% of attack flows through at its pre-committed threshold. Horizontal scaling of the full role stays Spark-bound (3.3\,flows/s) and the Spark standalone cluster reaches 8.7\,flows/s, so pipeline split remains our recommended default. Serving the same forest, ONNX Runtime is about $7{,}200\times$ faster than the deployed PySpark path with identical predictions, a concrete target for the next iteration of the serving layer. Future work includes TensorRT optimization, live-traffic validation, and federated retraining under drift~\cite{hernandezramos2025federated}.

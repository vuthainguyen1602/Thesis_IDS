\begin{abstract}
Deploying machine learning (ML) based intrusion detection on resource-constrained edge devices requires balancing accuracy, latency, and operational cost. We present a split-deployment IDS architecture across two NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super devices connected through Apache Kafka. The system implements a two-stage pipeline: (1) a lightweight autoencoder anomaly gate on Jetson~\#1 filters benign traffic, and (2) a PySpark \texttt{PipelineModel} classifier on Jetson~\#2 processes only suspicious flows. Central infrastructure (Kafka, PostgreSQL, InfluxDB, Grafana) runs on a host PC. We evaluate three distributed modes (pipeline split, horizontal Kafka consumer scaling, and a Spark standalone cluster) while streaming CICIDS2017 network flows. Following every flow from send to verdict, queueing included, pipeline split sustains 22 flows/s against 2.5 for a single node running the whole pipeline ($\approx$9$\times$), at an end-to-end p95 of about 7\,s and 0.60\,J per verdict against 3.04\,J. Most of the gain comes from decoupling the stages rather than from the second board: the gate run inside the classifier's process gains nothing, while the two stages as separate processes on one board sustain 17.8\,flows/s at 0.32\,J per verdict. The price is recall. Over all 10{,}000 replayed flows the deployed pipeline raises no false alarm (precision 100\%) but detects 69.5\% of attack flows (F1 0.82), and every miss is a flow the gate skipped. On replayed CSE-CIC-IDS2018 traffic the pipeline detects under 1\% of attacks and, because the gate forwards three times as many flows, cuts capacity to 8\,flows/s.

\keywords{Intrusion detection \and Edge computing \and Jetson Orin Nano Super \and Kafka \and PySpark \and Stream processing.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Network intrusion detection systems (IDS) increasingly must operate closer to data sources to reduce bandwidth and response time~\cite{shi2016edge,hamdan2020edge}. Recent work confirms that accurate detectors now fit on single-board hardware: ensemble and metaheuristic-optimized detectors have been demonstrated on IoT-edge platforms~\cite{baalaji2025ensemble,asghar2026hedid}, lightweight models have been designed for deployment on industrial control devices~\cite{zhang2026edgeics}, and NVIDIA Jetson-class boards have been used both for autoencoder-based IIoT detection~\cite{hasan2025iiot} and, on the Jetson Orin Nano specifically, for generative-AI-assisted traffic classification~\cite{diazgorrin2026jetson}. Such devices nonetheless offer limited CPU, memory, and thermal headroom, so a monolithic detector that classifies every flow saturates the board quickly.

Three gaps persist in this literature. First, the recent edge-IDS studies above evaluate a \emph{single} device: streaming middleware such as Kafka has been paired with Spark for distributed detection~\cite{kumar2025kafkaspark}, but the resulting pipelines are not measured on a multi-node cluster of constrained edge boards with explicit role separation, so it remains unclear how much a second board actually buys and in which distribution mode. Second, energy is now a design constraint in its own right at the edge~\cite{nassef2026tinyml}, yet throughput, latency percentiles, and per-inference energy are rarely reported together for one deployed system. Third, offline detection scores are increasingly under scrutiny: methodological pitfalls in security ML~\cite{arp2022dosdonts} and documented labeling and duplication errors in CICIDS2017~\cite{lanvin2023errors} mean that a model selected under a leaky protocol may be optimistic by several F1 points before it is ever deployed.

We address all three at once. A practical edge IDS must decide \emph{which} model to deploy (small, accurate, explainable, and selected under a leakage-aware protocol) and \emph{how} to distribute inference across nodes under streaming load. We therefore split detection into a lightweight autoencoder gate and a heavier PySpark classifier, place the two stages in different ways on Jetson Orin Nano Super boards, and measure each placement end to end. The building blocks (an autoencoder, Kafka, Spark MLlib) are established. We do not propose a new detector. The contribution is empirical: which placement pays off on real edge hardware, why, and what the gate costs in detection.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1) A placement study of a two-stage IDS (autoencoder gate, Spark classifier) on a two-board Jetson cluster: single node with and without the gate, pipeline split on one and on two boards, horizontal Kafka scaling, and a Spark standalone cluster, which shows that decoupling the stages, not the filter alone or the second board, delivers most of the gain;
(2) An end-to-end evaluation that follows every flow from send to verdict, reporting sustained capacity, latency percentiles with queueing included, energy per verdict, and the precision, recall and F1 of the deployed pipeline after gating, metrics that offline studies typically omit;
(3) A same-model engine baseline (PySpark, NumPy, ONNX Runtime) that locates the cost of serving with Spark on the board;
(4) Open, reproducible scripts integrated with a leakage-aware Spark ML training pipeline, so the deployed model is the one validated under a rigorous offline protocol. All code and configurations are publicly available\footnote{\url{https://github.com/vuthainguyen1602/Thesis_IDS} (branch \texttt{develop}).}.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Edge and on-device intrusion detection.} Edge computing pushes analytics closer to data sources to reduce latency and bandwidth~\cite{shi2016edge,hamdan2020edge}, and successive work has shrunk IDS models until they fit single-board hardware. Early systems such as RPiDS~\cite{sforzin2016rpids} showed that a complete detection stack could run on a Raspberry Pi at all. Recent studies instead optimize the detector itself, via deep ensemble stacking on IoT-edge platforms~\cite{baalaji2025ensemble}, metaheuristic hyper-parameter search combined with explainability~\cite{asghar2026hedid}, and compact models tailored to industrial control traffic~\cite{zhang2026edgeics}. NVIDIA Jetson boards are a recurring target: autoencoder-based feature learning reaches sub-millisecond inference on a Jetson Nano~\cite{hasan2025iiot}, and generative models have been run on the Jetson Orin Nano to classify traffic and synthesize training data at once~\cite{diazgorrin2026jetson}. These studies share a single-device assumption: the detector is benchmarked in isolation on one board, so how detection work should be placed across several boards, and what a second board actually buys, remain open.

\textbf{Streaming and distributed detection pipelines.} Kafka~\cite{kreps2011kafka} decouples producers from consumers and has become the de-facto ingest layer for streaming analytics, while Spark~\cite{zaharia2010spark} and its MLlib library~\cite{meng2016mllib} supply the distributed training and scoring primitives. Combining the two for intrusion detection is an established pattern. Kiran Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2025kafkaspark}, for instance, route Kafka streams into Spark classifiers for distributed detection. Such pipelines are conceived for datacenter-class resources, where an additional executor is cheap, whereas on an 8\,GB ARM board the JVM footprint is itself a design constraint, and the corresponding per-node CPU, memory, and energy traces are rarely published. More broadly, ML-based IDS surveys~\cite{khraisat2019survey,ferrag2020deep,vinayakumar2019deep} catalog detection accuracy across dozens of models but seldom report the end-to-end throughput or latency percentiles of a running deployment.

\textbf{Two-stage and split inference.} Anomaly-detection surveys~\cite{chandola2009anomaly,ruff2021unifying} systematize the family of detectors that model normal behavior alone. This property is what makes a cascaded design attractive, in which a cheap unsupervised stage filters the bulk of normal data and a costlier supervised stage adjudicates the remainder. Unsupervised deep detectors, deep belief networks~\cite{alrawashdeh2016toward} and autoencoders~\cite{lopez2017autoencoder,hasan2025iiot}, are the usual choice for the cheap stage, since they can be calibrated on benign traffic alone. Distributing detection across devices has so far been explored mainly along the federated-learning axis, where nodes collaborate during training while inference stays local~\cite{nguyen2019diot,hernandezramos2025federated}. Our split is orthogonal and concerns inference: the two stages are physically separated across boards and joined by a Kafka topic, so gate and classifier scale independently, and no prior work applies a PySpark MLlib classifier in this role on the NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super. Placement matters most for energy: even recent energy-aware edge IDS work finds per-sample inference on small boards too slow for strict real-time use~\cite{nassef2026tinyml}, and skipping the heavy stage is the most direct way to lower it.

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.} A parallel line of work questions how faithfully offline scores transfer to deployment. Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022dosdonts} catalog recurring pitfalls in security ML, and Lanvin et al.~\cite{lanvin2023errors} document labeling and duplication errors in CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018toward} that measurably inflate reported performance. Sarhan et al.~\cite{sarhan2022explainable} similarly show that the choice of standard feature set shapes both generalizability and explainability. Our offline selection responds by choosing a leakage-free SHAP Top-30 model~\cite{lundberg2017unified}, and the present paper deploys exactly that model, so the operational numbers reported here do not belong to a configuration that would fail a stricter offline audit. Concept drift~\cite{gama2014survey} lies outside the present scope and motivates the retraining direction we outline in the conclusion. Table~\ref{tab:related-work} positions our system against representative related work along the five axes above: multi-node role separation, streaming ingest, joint energy and latency reporting, leakage-aware model selection, and open code.

\subsection{Split Deployment Overview}

\subsection{Pipeline Roles}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anomaly gate:} a lightweight autoencoder + scaler over all 77 leak-free flow features. The MSE threshold is calibrated offline as a quantile (0.995) of a held-out benign validation split (10\% of benign training flows, never the test set), so the operating point is committed before evaluation.
  \item \textbf{Classifier:} a Random Forest PySpark \texttt{PipelineModel} over the leakage-free SHAP Top-30 feature set (Sect.~\ref{sec:edge-phase}).
  \item \textbf{Combined role:} gate and classifier in one process, used for horizontal scaling via Kafka consumer groups.
\end{itemize}
Each tier scores flows in batches of 20, or sooner once the oldest waiting flow has been held for 1\,s, so the classifier, which receives only a small share of traffic, never holds a partial batch indefinitely.

\subsection{Monitoring and Alerting}

Each node tags its metrics with a unique node identifier in InfluxDB and PostgreSQL. Alerts are emitted from a designated primary node only, avoiding duplicate notifications. Fig.~\ref{fig:grafana} shows the Grafana dashboard during the 500\,s run that replays all 10{,}000 CICIDS2017 test flows through the split deployment (Sect.~\ref{sec:benchmark-results}): prediction throughput, detected-attack count, inference latency, and per-node CPU/RAM utilization are all tracked in real time, alongside the most recent attack alerts persisted to PostgreSQL. Its CPU panel shows the load asymmetry: at peak load the gate node idles at 1.3\% CPU while the PySpark classifier node reaches 94.5\%, so the classifier dominates the compute budget.

\section{Implementation}

Training and model export run on a Spark standalone cluster (host PC as master, two Jetson Orin Nano Super devices as workers) using Apache Spark~\cite{zaharia2010spark} and its MLlib library~\cite{meng2016mllib}. The cluster topology is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:spark-cluster}. The host only coordinates (Spark master) and does not run an executor, so all reported training compute reflects the two homogeneous Jetson workers rather than the stronger host CPU. The edge stack is implemented in Python with configurable per-node roles. Every offline run logs its configuration (random seed, leakage/dedup switches, library versions) so each exported model is traceable to the exact pipeline that produced it.

\subsection{Offline Phase (Spark ML), Leakage-Aware}

In the offline selection stage, we evaluate four feature configurations (baseline with all features, RF Importance Top-30, SHAP Top-30, and PCA with $k$=40) under a fixed 80/20 train--test split, using eight Spark ML classifiers and statistical validation (repeated paired resampling and permutation tests). LightGBM is excluded because its SynapseML native libraries are x86\_64-only and cannot run on the ARM64 Jetson Orin Nano Super deployment target. The offline protocol is leakage-aware~\cite{arp2022dosdonts}: it removes information that would be unavailable at deployment time but that otherwise inflates test scores. Motivated by documented labeling and duplication errors in CICIDS2017~\cite{lanvin2023errors}, the label-leaking \texttt{destination\_port} feature is removed and flows that are identical on all feature columns are de-duplicated before splitting. This yields realistic F1 values (Table~\ref{tab:spark-edge-bridge}) rather than the near-perfect scores obtained when these leaks are left in place. Offline metrics constrain which exported model is worth deploying. They are not directly comparable to edge throughput or latency.

\subsection{Edge Phase (Jetson Orin Nano Super)}
\label{sec:edge-phase}

The exported \texttt{PipelineModel} (VectorAssembler, StandardScaler, classifier) is loaded on Jetson~\#2 with only the selected SHAP features, reducing assembly and inference cost relative to the 77-feature baseline. The deployed input vector contains the 30 SHAP-selected features. The ranking is computed after \texttt{destination\_port} has been removed as a label-leaking channel, consistent with the leakage-aware offline protocol. A lightweight autoencoder gate on Jetson~\#1 filters benign flows before they reach Spark inference, further lowering load on the classifier node.

We deploy the SHAP Top-30 feature set on Jetson~\#2 with a Random Forest classifier. SHAP Top-30 is the Random Forest's best feature set in Table~\ref{tab:spark-edge-bridge}: with 30 of 77 features it scores F1 0.9955 offline (0.9959 for the exported model), less than 0.002 below the best XGBoost score. Random Forest is also a native Spark ML estimator, so the exported \texttt{PipelineModel} loads on the ARM board without the native libraries that Spark's XGBoost integration requires. Sect.~\ref{sec:benchmark-results} then evaluates this choice under streaming load. The full four-method comparison and drift analysis are beyond the scope of this paper.

\section{Experimental Setup}

The testbed comprises two NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano Super Developer Kits and a host PC running Docker Compose, connected over a Wi-Fi LAN. Each Jetson board pairs a 6-core Arm Cortex-A78AE CPU with an NVIDIA Ampere GPU featuring 1024 CUDA and 32 Tensor cores, delivering roughly 67 TOPS, and is equipped with 8\,GB of LPDDR5 memory and a 256\,GB NVMe SSD. On the software side, streaming inference runs on PySpark 3.4.1 over Apache Kafka 3.5 (Confluent Platform 7.5.0), while InfluxDB and Grafana collect and visualize runtime metrics. The workload replays 10{,}000 flows sampled from the CICIDS2017 test split (15.2\% attacks, never seen in training). We compare a single node running the full pipeline, with and without the gate, against the three distributed modes A (pipeline split), B (horizontal Kafka scaling, full role on both boards) and C (Spark standalone cluster, host as master). A further placement, A1, runs the pipeline split on one board, with gate and classifier as two separate processes on Jetson~\#2. A1 was measured in a later session with the same boards, models, replay file and network.

Every verdict stores the flow's producer timestamp and replay row, so each flow sent during a load window is followed to its verdict wherever it is issued. We report \emph{sustained capacity}, the highest offered rate at which every flow sent in a 45\,s window receives its verdict within 10\,s of the window's end (about one PySpark batch); send-to-verdict latency p50/p95 over those flows, queueing included (the offset between the host and board clocks is measured before and after every window and subtracted); the gate-skip ratio and the confusion counts of the final verdicts computed from the flows themselves; and energy per verdict from \texttt{tegrastats} at the sustained rate. Each rate is run twice after a warm-up, with a fresh Kafka consumer group per run so no run inherits another's backlog. Because every window replays the sample from its first row, the capacity windows cover only its first few thousand flows. For the detection metrics, one additional 500\,s run of mode A at 20\,flows/s, below its sustained rate, replays all 10{,}000 flows once.

\textbf{Capacity.} Pipeline split sustains 22.4 flows/s, about $9\times$ the single node that runs the whole pipeline (2.5\,flows/s). The gate alone does not explain the gain: with the gate added to the single node, where gate and classifier run one after the other in the same process, it shows no measurable gain (2.4--2.5 verdicts/s at 3\,flows/s, the same as without the gate), because each batch still waits for a PySpark call that costs seconds regardless of how few flows reach it. Pipeline split decouples the two stages, so the gate keeps consuming while the classifier works through its share. Decoupling, not the second board, accounts for most of the gain: the same split as two processes on one board (A1) sustains 17.8\,flows/s, $7\times$ the single node, and the second board adds about a quarter on top. Horizontal scaling of the full role (gate off) stays Spark-bound at 3.3\,flows/s. The Spark standalone cluster sustains at least 8.7\,flows/s. At 20\,flows/s one of two repeats kept up (17.1\,flows/s) and the other did not, so its capacity lies between about 9 and 17\,flows/s, below pipeline split and with an extra host dependency.

\textbf{Latency.} At its sustained rate, pipeline split returns a median verdict in about 1\,s, dominated by gate-only verdicts. Flows forwarded to the classifier wait for a PySpark batch, and the p95 over all flows is 7.1\,s. Every mode shows a multi-second tail for the same reason: one PySpark batch on the board takes seconds regardless of its size.

\textbf{Energy efficiency.} Power is sampled with \texttt{tegrastats} (30\,s idle baseline, then the load window). Serving 18.4\,flows/s on two boards, pipeline split spends 0.60\,J per verdict against 3.04\,J for a single node at its 2\,flows/s, a $5\times$ reduction. On one board, A1 needs only 0.32\,J per verdict at 17.8\,flows/s, since the second board's idle draw disappears. The active (idle-subtracted) power rises by about 1.5\,W under load in every mode.

\textbf{Inference-engine baseline (cost of the unified stack).} We deploy a Spark \texttt{PipelineModel} on the edge for consistency with the distributed training phase: the same stack trains on the cluster and serves on the device. To quantify the price of that unification, we export the deployed forest to NumPy and ONNX Runtime and serve all three through the edge's own inference layer on the classifier node, batch size 10 (Table~\ref{tab:engines}). The three engines return identical predictions on every replayed flow. ONNX Runtime serves the same forest at about 10{,}100\,flows/s, about $7{,}200\times$ the PySpark path, at $<$0.01\% of its active energy per inference. The gap motivates exporting the \texttt{PipelineModel} to ONNX/TensorRT for serving while retaining Spark for training. Wiring the ONNX session into the streaming pipeline is left as future work. A batch-size sweep on the same board shows the PySpark cost is a fixed per-call overhead (7.1--7.4\,s from batch 10 to 1{,}000), not the trees. Larger batches narrow the gap to about $27\times$ at 10{,}000 flows, but at 20\,flows/s collecting such a batch takes over 8 minutes.

\textbf{Gate trade-off.} The anomaly gate is tunable rather than a single fixed point: sweeping its threshold trades attack recall against the fraction of flows offloaded from Spark (Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}). At the pre-committed quantile of 0.995 it forwards 68.1\% of attack flows at a 0.49\% false-positive rate while skipping 89.4\% of all flows offline. Flows the gate skips are final, so the gate bounds the system's recall. Scoring all 77 features matters: restricted to the classifier's 30 SHAP features, the same gate forwards only 44.8\% of attack flows at the same quantile, because a subset chosen to separate the classes is not the one that exposes anomalies.

\section{Discussion}

Two findings qualify the design. First, decoupling the stages matters more than the filter or the hardware: the gate in the classifier's process gains nothing, while a second board adds only a quarter more capacity at twice the energy per verdict, so one board running the split is the more efficient choice and a second board buys headroom. Second, the throughput is paid for in recall, and Table~\ref{tab:e2e} shows the gate's skips are the pipeline's only errors, so an operator should trade recall for capacity knowingly along the curve of Fig.~\ref{fig:gate}.

\textbf{Dependence on the host.} The boards run all detection, but Kafka, PostgreSQL, InfluxDB and Grafana run on the host PC, so the deployment is not autonomous: flows enter through the host's broker and verdicts are stored there. If the host fails, detection stops, even though both boards remain healthy. With the PySpark classifier the host is not the bottleneck: the boards saturate first, and a round trip from host to board averages 4--9\,ms on the Wi-Fi LAN. The coupling still bounds scaling: every added board is one more consumer of the same broker and one more writer to the same database, one insert per verdict. A field deployment would therefore move ingest to the edge: a broker on, or next to, the board that receives the flow export, with verdicts buffered locally and forwarded to a central store in batches, so that detection keeps running when the uplink or the host is lost. Kafka's replication would let such a broker be spread over the boards themselves. These unmeasured variants trade the JVM broker's memory on an 8\,GB board against independence from the host.

\textbf{Beyond CICIDS2017.} To test the deployed system on another network, we replayed 10{,}000 flows sampled from the deduplicated, leak-free test split of CSE-CIC-IDS2018~\cite{csecicids2018} (12.8\% attacks, columns mapped to the CICIDS2017 names) through the same pipeline split, keeping the models trained on CICIDS2017 (Table~\ref{tab:xdataset}). The gate, calibrated on CICIDS2017 benign traffic, forwards 30\% of the 2018 flows instead of 11\%, so the classifier receives three times the load and capacity falls from 22.4 to about 8\,flows/s. Detection collapses to a recall of 0.8\%, and the classifier itself, not only the gate, misses almost every 2018 attack. An offline study with the same model family (class-weighted Random Forest, same leakage-aware protocol, shared features, 18 runs) agrees with earlier inter-dataset studies~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset,cantone2024crossdataset}: across datasets AUC-PR falls to 0.48 (2017$\rightarrow$2018) and 0.19 (2018$\rightarrow$2017), while training on 1\% of the target dataset's labelled flows restores F1 to 0.92 and 0.99. A new network therefore needs the gate recalibrated on local benign traffic and the classifier retrained.

\textbf{Limitations.} Benchmarks are obtained on a two-node lab cluster over Wi-Fi. Wired networking and larger clusters may shift latency tails. Capacity is bounded by the PySpark classifier, whose per-batch cost of several seconds sets both the tail latency and the ceiling. The $\sim$67-TOPS GPU remains unexploited by the CPU-only pipeline. The gate bounds recall, and its threshold is calibrated on benign training data only. Energy figures subtract a single pre-run idle baseline, so within-run drift in background power is not captured. Live traffic and deliberately evasive (adversarial) traffic remain untested, and the host-side services were not moved to the edge.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a reproducible distributed edge IDS for Jetson Orin Nano Super clusters that pairs Kafka streaming with PySpark inference and deploys a model validated under a leakage-aware offline protocol. Measured from send to verdict with queueing included, placing a lightweight autoencoder gate and the Spark classifier on separate boards sustains 22.4 flows/s against 2.5 for a single node running the whole pipeline ($\approx$9$\times$), at an end-to-end p95 of about 7\,s and 0.60\,J per verdict against 3.04\,J. The gain requires decoupling the stages: the same gate run in the classifier's process brings no measurable gain, whereas the two stages as separate processes on one board already sustain 17.8\,flows/s at 0.32\,J per verdict. It is paid for in recall. Over all 10{,}000 replayed flows the pipeline raises no false alarm but detects 69.5\% of attacks (F1 0.82), and every miss is a flow the gate skipped. Horizontal scaling and the Spark standalone cluster stay below pipeline split. On CSE-CIC-IDS2018 replay the gate forwards three times as many flows and the pipeline detects under 1\% of attacks, so a new network needs a recalibrated gate and a retrained classifier. Serving the same forest, ONNX Runtime is about $7{,}200\times$ faster than the deployed PySpark path at its batch size of 10, with identical predictions, a concrete target for the next iteration of the serving layer. Future work includes TensorRT optimization, live-traffic validation, and federated retraining under drift~\cite{hernandezramos2025federated}.
